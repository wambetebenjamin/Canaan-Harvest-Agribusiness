Managu, terere and saga spent decades being treated as village food while spinach got the supermarket shelf. That is a loss, because all three are more nutritious and more interesting to cook.

## What they actually are

- **Managu** — African nightshade. Slightly bitter, dark green, holds its shape when cooked. High in iron and calcium.
- **Terere** — amaranth greens. Milder, softer, cooks down dramatically. Very high in vitamin A.
- **Saga** — African spider plant. Peppery, tender, best cooked briefly.

## The preparation that matters

All three benefit from the same start: **wash in plenty of cold water, then drain properly**. Wet greens steam instead of fry, and you lose the texture that makes them worth eating.

Do not shred them fine. A rough chop holds better.

## Recipe 1 — Managu with onion and cream

The classic. Fry a diced onion until soft, add a crushed garlic clove, then a kilo of chopped managu. Cook for six minutes, stirring. Finish with two tablespoons of cream or a spoon of ghee and a pinch of salt.

The cream takes the edge off the bitterness without hiding it.

## Recipe 2 — Terere with groundnut

Terere and groundnuts are a natural pairing. Simmer chopped terere for five minutes, drain, then fold through three tablespoons of groundnut paste loosened with warm water. Add a chopped tomato at the start if you want acidity.

This is rich, and it works as a main with ugali.

## Recipe 3 — Saga with eggs

Saga cooks in three minutes. Wilt it in a hot pan, push to one side, scramble two eggs in the same pan, then fold together. Salt, white pepper, done.

Good breakfast, and a very cheap one.

## Recipe 4 — Mixed greens with dhania and lemon

Take equal parts managu, terere and spinach. Wilt all three, then dress off the heat with chopped dhania, lemon juice and a little olive oil. Serve warm, not hot.

This is the version we recommend to hotel kitchens — it plates well and holds under a heat lamp.

## A note on the nutrition claims

Indigenous greens are genuinely nutrient-dense, but the biggest nutritional mistake is **overcooking them to a pulp**. Water-soluble vitamins do not survive a forty-minute boil. Six to eight minutes is where flavour and nutrition both peak.

> If you are buying for a restaurant, ask for managu cut the same morning. A day in the crate and the bitterness climbs.
