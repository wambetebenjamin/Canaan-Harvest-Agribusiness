An off-take agreement is a promise: you grow it, we buy it. That is genuinely valuable when seed, fertiliser and labour all have to be paid for months before a shilling comes in.

But the value depends entirely on seven clauses. If any of them is missing or vague, the net is not there.

## The seven clauses to check

### 1. Volume — and what happens if you cannot deliver

A volume commitment with no shortfall provision is a one-way promise. Look for language that says what happens if disease or weather cuts your yield by forty per cent. In our agreements, a documented shortfall reduces the commitment rather than triggering a penalty.

### 2. Grade standard, in writing, with numbers

"The buyer's specification" is not a standard. A usable standard says: *12 kg crate, 8–10 cm length, no yellowing, no insect damage, third-cut or later.* If the grade standard can change arbitrarily, your price can too.

### 3. Price, or a mechanism that produces one

A fixed price for a whole season protects you against a price crash and costs you a price rally. An indexed price does the opposite. Either can be fair — but "to be agreed at harvest" is neither. It hands all the power to whoever has the cash that week.

### 4. Payment terms with dates

"Payment on delivery" and "payment within 30 days of invoice" are different things. Get a number of days and a consequence for late payment.

### 5. Who pays for transport

Collection from the farm is a real cost — often 10–15 per cent of the produce value on a bad road. Make it explicit rather than assumed.

### 6. Inputs and their recovery

If the buyer supplies seed or fertiliser, read how it is recovered. Deducted from the first harvest payment at what valuation? Is there interest? This is the clause most often used to quietly reduce your effective price.

### 7. Termination

How does the agreement end? A fair one lets either side exit at the end of a season with notice before the next planting. One that only the buyer can terminate is not a partnership.

## The test we apply to ourselves

We only ask a partner to plant against **confirmed demand**. Nothing enters the crop plan before a buyer line exists for it. And if the market price rises above the agreed price, we pass through the increase under the mechanism in the agreement rather than holding the farmer to a stale figure.

That last part costs us money. It is also why our partners plant with us a second season.

## Red flags

- No written grade standard.
- Price left open.
- Buyer-supplied inputs deducted at an undisclosed valuation.
- Penalties for under-delivery with no relief for crop failure.
- Exclusive supply with no guaranteed minimum volume.
- No termination clause at all.

> A contract that only works in a good year is not insurance. It is a loan against your next harvest.
