A hotel kitchen in Westlands needs its produce to arrive between 6am and 9am, cold, correct, and in a form the kitchen can use without rework. Behind that ordinary expectation sits a supply chain that starts the previous morning, two hundred kilometres away.

Here is the route.

## The day before, 09:00 — the order lands

The kitchen confirms tomorrow's list: sukuma wiki, tomatoes, hoho, dhania, and a fixed spec for each. Grade, pack size, and in some cases the exact cut.

## 14:00 — the crop plan is checked

The quantity is matched against what is standing in the ground and what is booked against other buyers. If there is a gap, the kitchen hears about it today, not tomorrow morning at 5am.

## 05:30 next morning — harvest begins

Cut in the cool hours. Crates only, never sacks. Into shade within the hour.

## 07:30 — into the packhouse

Washed, graded by size, colour and firmness, then cooled. Leafy greens go to 4°C. Anything failing the grade is diverted rather than blended into the order — this is the single decision that keeps our buyer-end rejection rate under two per cent.

## 04:30 — vans leave the packhouse

Before dawn, because a Nairobi route at 6am is a different proposition to a Nairobi route at 8am. Fixed routes, fixed stops, a named driver on every one.

## 06:00–09:00 — the delivery window

This is the part buyers actually judge you on. Not the price, not the farm story — whether the crate was on the loading bay when the kitchen opened.

## What breaks the chain

| Failure point | What it costs |
| --- | --- |
| Harvesting too late in the day | 2–3 days of shelf life |
| A sack instead of a crate | Bruised leaf, rejected at the door |
| Loading warm produce | Van cannot recover the temperature |
| An unplanned route change | MIssed window, kitchen runs short at service |
| Quietly substituting a line | Trust, permanently |

## Why we publish this

Because most produce supply problems are not mysteries. They are a specific decision made worse at a specific hour. Naming the hour is how you fix it.

If you run a kitchen and want a supplier who will tell you on Tuesday that Thursday's delivery is at risk, rather than on Thursday morning after it fails — that is the conversation to have.

> We would rather lose an order to an honest warning than keep it with a surprise.
