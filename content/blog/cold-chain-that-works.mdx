Most cold-chain advice assumes a packhouse with a blast chiller and an unbroken fleet of refrigerated trucks. That advice is useless on a four-acre plot in Machakos with a borehole and a pickup.

Here is what actually moves the needle, in order of how much quality it saves per shilling spent.

## The two-hour rule

Cut leafy greens at 6am and leave them in the sun until noon, and you have lost most of the shelf life you spent three months growing. The single highest-return intervention is not refrigeration — it is **getting the crop into shade immediately**.

We ask every partner farm for three things before anything else:

1. A shaded area within 200 metres of the field, even if it is a simple pole-frame with a shade net.
2. Harvest in the cool hours. Cut between 05:30 and 08:00.
3. Crates, not sacks. A woven sack abrades the leaf and traps field heat.

That alone typically adds two to three days of shelf life.

## Field heat is the real enemy

Produce comes out of the ground warm. Warm produce respires faster, wilts faster and spoils faster. You do not need a chiller to fix this — you need **air movement and evaporative cooling**:

- Stack crates with gaps, never solid blocks.
- Dampen the floor, not the produce. Evaporating water pulls heat out of the air.
- Keep the shade structure open on the prevailing-wind side.

On our Machakos plots this brings leafy greens from 28°C at harvest to around 18°C within 90 minutes, with no electricity at all.

## Where refrigeration earns its cost

Cold-chain equipment is worth buying when it protects the *last mile*, not the first. Specifically:

- **A pre-cooling step at the packhouse.** Taking greens to 4°C before loading is what lets a van hold temperature on a hot Nairobi afternoon.
- **Insulated boxes, not a refrigerated truck**, for small volumes. A well-insulated box with a couple of frozen gel packs will hold temperature for a four-hour route. A refrigerated truck is a capital expense most farms cannot justify.
- **A temperature logger.** A cheap one in each consignment turns "it felt cold" into evidence. This matters enormously when a buyer disputes quality.

## What we measure

We hold leafy greens at 2–4°C and monitor it. Our measured rejection rate at the buyer end is under two per cent, and most of what does get rejected is damaged in the buyer's own handling rather than ours.

If you are a farm partner, this is the standard we train to and the standard the quarterly grading review checks against.

> Cold chain is not a piece of equipment. It is a habit of not letting the crop sit in the sun.
