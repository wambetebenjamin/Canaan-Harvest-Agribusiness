Buying out of season in Kenya means buying imported, stored, or forced. All three cost more and taste of less. Buying *in* season means produce that was harvested this week, at a price the farmer can live with.

## Why the calendar matters more here than in Europe

Kenya does not have four seasons. We have **long rains** (March–May), **cool dry** (June–August), **short rains** (October–December) and **hot dry** (January–February). Each shifts what grows well, what it costs, and what quality you can realistically expect.

A tomato in February and a tomato in July are not the same product, even from the same farm.

## What is strong right now

| Category | In season | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leafy greens | Sukuma wiki, managu, terere, spinach | Best quality and lowest price of the year in the cool dry months |
| Fruiting vegetables | Tomatoes, hoho, courgette | Peak flavour, but watch for blossom-end rot in hot spells |
| Root and tuber | Carrots, beetroot, potatoes, nduma | Stores well, so availability is steady |
| Fruit | Avocado, passion, mango (late), banana | Avocado export grade peaks June–August |

## What to avoid, and when

- **Tomatoes in the short-rains peak.** Supply floods, quality collapses. If you are buying volume for a restaurant, this is the month to lock a fixed spec with a supplier rather than shopping the spot price.
- **Leafy greens after heavy rain.** Splitting and rapid spoilage. Insist on cold-chain delivery or buy smaller and more often.
- **Out-of-season fruit for a menu.** Mango in February is either imported or cold-stored, and a chef can taste both.

## A buying rule that works

If it is cheap, plentiful and delicious, it is in season. If it is expensive and slightly disappointing, it is not. That single test will outperform any printed chart — including this one.

> Buy the year, not the week. The farms that supply you well in the lean months are the ones you paid properly in the good ones.
